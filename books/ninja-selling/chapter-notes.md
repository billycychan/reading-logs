# Chapter Notes

## Principle 1: Personal Mastery

### Chapter 1: The Ninja Mind-Set

The opening chapter distinguishes a player from a victim. A victim focuses on
circumstances, limitations, and what others have done; a player accepts
responsibility for choosing a constructive response. Customers perceive this
mind-set through the salesperson's demeanor and are more inclined to work with
someone who appears positive, capable, and responsible.

Maria Vitale illustrates the shift. After losing her marriage and home, she
was living with her mother and had developed a victim mentality. She realized
that her energy discouraged potential customers. By adopting the Ninja morning
routine and deliberately changing her mind-set, she changed her behavior and
relationships, completed nine transactions in one month, and purchased a home
for her family. The chapter's central claim is that changes in external results
begin with changes in internal state.

### Chapter 2: Good Vibrations

Kendall describes people as senders and receivers of emotional energy.
Thoughts and feelings determine the frequency a person broadcasts, while
others experience it as the person's vibe. Customers may not name the emotion
directly, but they often detect confidence, fear, anger, scarcity, or genuine
interest.

Selling techniques are therefore insufficient when the salesperson projects
desperation or negativity. Emotional patterns can become self-reinforcing:
negative energy damages interactions and produces more negative expectations,
while a deliberate change in thoughts and feelings can begin an upward cycle.
Controlling thoughts influences feelings; controlling feelings influences the
vibe sent to others.

### Chapter 3: Controlling Your Emotional Energy

Emotional energy is organized into four quadrants. High-positive energy
includes focused confidence, inspiration, and positive expectation;
low-positive energy includes calm, safety, and contentment. High-negative
energy includes fear, anger, and frustration, while low-negative energy
includes guilt, sadness, and depression.

Peak performance takes place in the high-positive quadrant, but the ideal is
not frantic enthusiasm. It is a calm, attentive, nonanxious presence that
notices subtle changes in the customer. Players still experience negative
episodes but use response-ability to return to the positive side. Peak
performance depends on trusting a proven process, practicing the required
skills, and using a pre-performance ritual.

### Chapter 4: Rituals

A ritual is performed immediately before an important activity to create the
desired state. A sales professional might use music, controlled breathing,
meditation, visualization, affirmations, or mental rehearsal before a customer
meeting or negotiation.

Rituals can also interrupt negative states. Gratitude, exercise, uplifting
music, nature, learning, recreation, positive companions, and mentors can help
someone move back toward positive energy. Kendall warns against short-term
escapes such as substance use, compulsive consumption, or joining others in
mutual negativity. Because high-positive energy cannot be sustained
continuously, healthy performance alternates between low-positive recovery and
high-positive execution.

### Chapter 5: The Ninja Morning Routine

Routines shape life through repetition. The Ninja morning routine contains
four practices: gratitude, positive reading, affirmations or visualization,
and two personal notes. Gratitude develops positive humility; positive reading
provides constructive input; visualization directs attention toward desired
goals; and personal notes strengthen relationships by thanking,
congratulating, or encouraging others.

The routine should be completed before email, news, or outside demands capture
the day's attention. Its power comes from consistency and compounding rather
than complexity or occasional intensity.

### Chapter 6: What You Focus On Expands

The Reticular Activating System is described as an attentional search engine.
It filters the environment and brings forward what a person has programmed as
important. Someone considering a particular car suddenly notices it
everywhere; similarly, a trained salesperson notices life changes and
opportunities that were previously invisible.

The RAS can focus on what a person wants and values or on what the person fears
and wants to avoid. Negative instructions still keep attention on the unwanted
object, as when a golfer thinks only about avoiding a sand trap. Goals should
therefore focus on the desired destination. A written life list of what one
wants to have, do, become, and give provides constructive targets. The
governing principle is both a promise and a warning: what you focus on expands.

### Chapter 7: Learning to Run Your Brain

Kendall presents three interacting mental systems. The conscious brain handles
present awareness. The nonconscious brain records experience and develops the
program underlying self-image, self-talk, self-esteem, and beliefs. The
creative nonconscious acts like an autopilot, encouraging behavior consistent
with that program.

The internal little voice reveals whether a goal fits the existing program.
Immediate rejection of an ambitious goal indicates conflict with established
beliefs. Conscious goal setting is therefore not enough; the nonconscious
program must be changed so habitual behavior supports the goal. Kendall uses
his recovery from childhood encephalitis and the insecurity caused by its
effects to illustrate the possibility of changing limiting self-programming.

### Chapter 8: My Results Formula

The Results Formula connects thoughts, beliefs, actions, and results. Repeated
thoughts form beliefs about oneself and the world. People act consistently
with those beliefs, and their actions produce results. Changing a target
without changing the supporting beliefs and behavior often fails.

Accepting the formula requires responsibility for controllable thoughts,
beliefs, and actions. When events cannot be controlled, a person still has
response-ability: the ability to choose a response. Jack Taylor illustrates
the idea by continuing to make calls after breaking his neck immediately
before beginning his real-estate career, ultimately producing exceptional
first-year results.

### Chapter 9: Creating Your Future

Five forces shape the person one becomes: the people one meets, the places one
goes, the material one reads or consumes, one's habits, and one's thoughts.
Friends and mentors influence expectations; travel expands the perceptual map;
constructive media improves mental input; habits compound; and visualization
and affirmation direct self-image and self-talk.

The future is not portrayed as the result of one dramatic decision. It emerges
from the relationships, environments, information, habits, and thoughts
selected repeatedly in the present. Distraction is therefore a major enemy of
mastery.

### Chapter 10: Eight Key Steps to Proper Programming

Effective mental programming follows eight conditions. A goal must belong to
the individual, be positive and measurable, use the first person, and be
stated in the present tense. It should be visualized through one's own eyes,
include sensory detail, be practiced daily for thirty days, and be reinforced
by an environmental anchor such as an image or dream board.

The wording matters because a future-oriented statement about escaping a
problem reinforces wanting rather than having. A formulation such as enjoying
financial freedom presents a positive, measurable state as a current
experience.

### Chapter 11: The Power of Affirmations

A useful affirmation is positive, specific, personal, and present-tense.
Kendall recommends writing it twenty-five times daily for thirty days while
experiencing the feeling associated with its fulfillment. The little voice can
be used as a test: resistance suggests the belief has not yet entered the
program, while growing agreement suggests that habitual behavior may begin to
align with it.

Mary Lou York's income affirmation eventually changed her internal dialogue
and prompted behavior consistent with her target. Kendall compares the
practice to mental push-ups: occasional repetition creates little strength,
while a brief, consistent daily practice can produce change.

### Chapter 12: The Power of Focus

The Net Forward Energy Ratio compares productive positive energy directed
toward a goal with doubt, distraction, and divided attention pulling away from
it. A 60/40 ratio produces only 1.5 units of forward energy; 80/20 produces 4;
and 90/10 produces 9. Small gains in alignment can therefore generate much
larger gains in movement.

Negative energy includes more than pessimism. Competing priorities,
interruptions, and partial attention also create drag. The formula can run in
reverse, so a small loss of focus may sharply reduce performance. Principle 1
ends by calling for goals, beliefs, energy, attention, habits, and action to
move in the same direction.
